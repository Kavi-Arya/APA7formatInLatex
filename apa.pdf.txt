Introduction
The psychodynamic approach focuses on the un-
conscious mind, right? So it’s not just about what
we consciously think or feel but also the underly-
ing stuff that influences our behavior without us
realizing it. That makes sense because sometimes
people do things they can’t explain, and this theory
would say it’s due to these unconscious drives.
Defense mechanisms are another important part.
I think Freud talked about repression a lot—like
how we push unwanted thoughts out of conscious-
ness. But there are others too, like projection or
denial.
These help protect the ego from stress
and anxiety but can also cause problems if they’re
overused.
Freud’s structure of personality is something
else to cover. The id, which is all about basic de-
sires and operates on the pleasure principle. Then
the ego, which deals with reality and mediates
between the id and superego.
And finally, the
superego, which incorporates societal morals and
ideals. I should explain each part clearly.
Oh, and there are different stages of psycho-
sexual development in Freud’s theory: oral, anal,
phallic, latency, and genital.
Each stage has its
own focus and potential issues if fixation happens.
For example, someone who is orally fixated might
smoke or overeat as an adult.
That shows how
early experiences shape later behavior.
Carl Jung added the concept of the collective
unconscious with archetypes—universal symbols
like the mother or hero that influence us all. And
Erik Erikson’s psychosocial stages focus on social
and cultural factors, looking at different challenges
at various life stages, such as trust vs. mistrust in
infancy or identity vs. role confusion in adoles-
cence.
The therapeutic method in psychodynamic the-
ory is psychoanalysis, which uses techniques
like free association to uncover unconscious
thoughts. Dream analysis was also a big part of
it—interpreting dreams to understand hidden de-
